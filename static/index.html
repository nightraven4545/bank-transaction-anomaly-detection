Transaction anomaly detector

Ranks payments by how unusual they are, explains every alert in plain words, and sizes the review queue to what a fraud team can actually check. Tested on real fraud labels and a real bank's ledger, and running live on Washington DC's public card spending.

Open the dashboard How it works

Fraud is rare, labels come late, and reviewers are few

No labels to learn from

Most fraud is confirmed weeks later, if ever. So this model never sees a fraud label. It ranks payments by how unusual they are, and labels are only used afterwards to grade it.

A fixed review budget

A fraud team can check perhaps 1 to 2% of payments. Alerts fill that budget from the top, instead of using a probability cut-off that could flag far more than anyone can review.

Bank data stays private

Privacy law and the risk of re-identifying people keep customer transactions out of public hands. So this project uses the most real public sources there are, and says which is which.

Follow one payment through the model

The most unusual of the DC payments, step by step. The same view opens for any alert in the dashboard.

  1. The payment

    Five columns arrive: date, agency, merchant, merchant category and amount. No cardholder is named, so each payment is compared with its agency and its category.

  2. Features put it in context

    Each feature is built so that higher means more unusual. The dot shows where this payment sits among all of them; the grey band is the middle half.

  3. Three detectors, three ideas of unusual

    Each detector scores every payment. Scores are in standard deviations above a typical payment, and #1 is the most unusual.

  4. Measure, then pick one

    Averaging the three sounds safer, so it was tested against each detector alone on real fraud labels. LOF breaks on data with many identical payments and drags the average down, so Isolation Forest, the simplest model that won every test, sets the order. KNN and LOF stay as second opinions.

    Share of 492 real card frauds caught when reviewing the top 1% of payments.

  5. Capacity sets the queue

    The queue is sized to what the team can review, not to a probability threshold.

  6. A reason a person can check

    Reasons come from the features where the payment ranks highest. A flag is a reason to look, not a verdict.

    Does it work?

    Two tests. One on real fraud labels, which the detectors never see. One on planted frauds, which shows which kinds it catches and which it misses.

    On real fraud: 284,807 card payments

    Card payments made by European cardholders in September 2013, with fraud confirmed by the card company. The labels only grade the ranking.

    Show as table

    On planted frauds, by kind

    The bank demo data has no labels, so 27 synthetic frauds of three kinds are planted into it and the test is run 20 times: account takeovers (4 to 5 logins, 3 to 6 times the amount), big-ticket buys (5 to 10 times the account's usual) and balance drains (90 to 100% of the money).

      The data, and why it looks like this

      Banks are bound by secrecy and data-protection law (India's DPDP Act 2023, the EU's GDPR, the US GLBA). Even anonymised payment trails can identify people from a few dates and shops, and fraud labels teach fraudsters what gets caught. So public data is either old and anonymised, deliberately obscured, published by governments about their own spending, or synthetic. This project uses one of each and labels them.

      Credit-card fraud, ULB and Worldline real, labelled

      284,807 card payments by European cardholders over two days in September 2013, 492 of them confirmed fraud. The card company replaced the original fields with 28 anonymous components for privacy, so this set grades the method and is not shown row by row. Downloaded from OpenML when the site is built, never stored here. Open Database License; Dal Pozzolo et al. 2015.

      Uses: amount + 28 components

      Czech bank ledger, PKDD'99 real bank, anonymised

      An anonymised ledger from a real Czech bank, released for the PKDD'99 Discovery Challenge (Berka): about a million transactions from 4,500 accounts, 1993 to 1998. The dashboard uses the full histories of 40 random accounts ( withdrawals, in Czech crowns). The same recipe scores uploaded bank statements. Mirrored for research for over 20 years without an explicit licence; cited here, source.

      Uses: withdrawal · share of money available · × account's usual · days since last withdrawal

      Washington DC government cards real, unlabelled

      DC publishes every payment made with a government purchase card. The dashboard uses the most recent ( to ): agencies, merchants. No cardholder is named. Refunds, dispute re-bills and an "Unknown New Agency" placeholder were removed. opendata.dc.gov, CC BY 4.0.

      Uses: amount · × agency's typical purchase · × typical for the category · how rare the category is for the agency

      Bank transactions demo synthetic

      generated bank transactions from Kaggle (Vala Khorasani, Apache-2.0). It has the fields a bank would log, like login attempts and session length, which makes it a good teaching set, but it is not real and has no fraud labels. Used for the planted-fraud test and the original DataCamp project.

      Uses: amount · session length · login attempts · amount ÷ balance left · × account's usual

      What the demo data could not tell us

      • So the model uses behavioural features only. Age and occupation describe the customer, not the payment, and are left out on purpose.

      Your bank statement

      Export a statement as CSV from net banking and upload it in the dashboard. Withdrawals are compared with your own history: size, share of the balance, and gaps between payments. It is scored in memory and never stored or logged.

      Limits and responsible use

      What the results do not show

      • The card data is from 2013 and hides its fields. It shows the ranking works on real fraud, not which fields mattered.
      • The DC and Czech data have no fraud labels. Their alerts are unusual payments, not proven wrongdoing.
      • Planted frauds encode our own idea of fraud, so that test measures "catches these patterns", not real-world recall.
      • Per-account baselines are noisy when an account has only a few payments.

      How it should be used

      • A flag is a reason to look, never an automatic block.
      • No demographic features: age, occupation and wealth are never inputs.
      • Every alert carries a reason a person can check.
      • Uploads are scored in memory and never stored or logged.
      • The queue is sized to review capacity, because every false alarm costs a real customer time.
